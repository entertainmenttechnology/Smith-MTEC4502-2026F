# Radar Chart Construction and Recursive Competency Assessment

**For MTEC 4502 – Career and Portfolio Seminar**

---

## Why use a radar chart in career planning?

A radar chart is useful when a role, discipline, or career direction involves multiple interdependent capabilities. It helps students visualize the shape of their readiness rather than treating skills as a single vague idea such as "creative," "technical," or "professional."

The goal is not to create a decorative diagram. The goal is to turn a career target into a set of observable, assessable skills linked to evidence, action, and growth.

This guide introduces a recursive model: a radar chart does not stop at one level. A student may begin with a role-level chart and then break selected axes into more precise sub-competencies.

---

## Core concept: recursive radar charts

### Level 1: Role-level radar

The first radar chart identifies the most essential competencies for a target role, discipline, or job family.

Examples:
- Game Designer
- Technical Artist
- UX Researcher
- Media Producer
- AI Workflow Designer
- Sound Designer

A role-level radar usually includes 5–8 major competencies. It should combine both hard and soft competencies, because most professional roles require some mix of technical execution, workflow knowledge, collaboration, communication, judgment, and adaptability.

Example axes might include:
- Creative Vision
- Technical Execution
- Collaboration
- Research and Analysis
- Communication
- Workflow and Production
- Adaptability

These axes should be generated from evidence rather than from a vague personal impression. Students should research job postings, analyze professional resumes and portfolios, compare role expectations across sources, and identify patterns in what employers, collaborators, or professionals in the field repeatedly value.

This chart answers the question: What matters most for this direction?

### Level 2: Sub-radar for a key axis

Once a student identifies a major competency, they can create a second radar chart for that one axis.

Example:
- Main role: Game Designer
- Major axis: Technical Design
- Sub-radar axes: Systems Thinking, Prototype Iteration, Balance and Mechanics, Playtesting, Collaboration with Engineers, UX Evaluation

This answers the question: What does this competency actually include?

Deeper sub-radars are not required for every competency. They are optional and should be used when a decision needs more clarity, a skill is high-risk, or the rubric would otherwise remain too vague to act on. If the broader chart already gives enough actionable information, a deeper layer may not be necessary.

### Level 3: Tool, platform, or functional decomposition

The recursive process can continue further. A competency may be broken down by function, application, or disciplinary subset.

Example:
- Main role: Game Designer
- Major axis: 3D Modeling
- Sub-radar axes: Modeling, Topology, UV Mapping, Retopology, Texturing, Asset Optimization, File Management, Pipeline Integration

Or:
- Main role: Media Producer
- Major axis: Workflow and Production
- Sub-radar axes: Scheduling, Collaboration, Asset Tracking, Budgeting, Communication, Quality Control

This answers the question: What specific capabilities sit inside the larger competency?

## Rule: use observable competencies, not vague labels

A radar chart only works if each axis is measurable.

Avoid vague labels such as:
- creative
- professional
- good communicator
- strong technical ability
- experienced

Instead, define a competency in terms of observable behavior.

For example, replace:
- "Creative" with "Develops concept directions informed by audience needs and project constraints"
- "Communication" with "Explains project decisions and tradeoffs to collaborators using clear evidence"
- "Technical skill" with "Builds and tests functional prototypes using current tools and workflow standards"

A competency should describe what a person can do, not how they feel about their work.

## Building a role-level radar chart

### Step 1: Choose the target role or discipline

Start with a specific role, job family, or path.

Examples:
- Technical artist for games
- Interactive media designer
- UX researcher in a product team
- Producer for digital media or events
- Sound designer for immersive media

The more specific the target, the more useful the chart becomes.

### Step 2: Identify 5–8 essential competencies

Choose the capabilities most likely to matter for the target role. These should reflect:
- job descriptions,
- industry expectations,
- professional portfolios,
- research on the field,
- and student strengths/gaps.

Students should include a mix of hard and soft competencies. Hard competencies may include tools, workflows, technical execution, analysis, or production skills. Soft competencies may include collaboration, communication, judgment, adaptability, problem framing, or stakeholder coordination.

A useful strategy is to gather evidence from several sources: job postings, resume examples, portfolio case studies, interviews, and related professional materials. The final chart should reflect recurring patterns, not just one-off preferences.

### Step 3: Define each axis in measurable terms

For each competency, write a short statement that explains what would count as evidence of competence.

Example:

| Competency | Observable definition |
|------------|-----------------------|
| Technical Execution | Produces working prototypes or assets that meet clear project constraints |
| Collaboration | Communicates with team members and integrates feedback into iterative revisions |
| Research and Analysis | Uses evidence from job posts, interviews, and field research to refine a strategy |
| Production Workflow | Organizes timelines, tools, and deliverables in a consistent and efficient process |

### Step 4: Rate current level and target level

Use a 1–5 scale or a comparable rubric. Each score should correspond to observable evidence.

Example rating scale:
- 1 = Not yet demonstrated
- 2 = Emerging / minimal evidence
- 3 = Demonstrated at a basic level
- 4 = Consistent and strong
- 5 = Advanced / can teach or lead others
- N/A = Not assessed / insufficient evidence yet

This last option matters: missing evidence should remain “Not assessed,” not be treated as if it were a low ability score. A blank or unassessed rating is a gap in evidence, not a judgment of capability.

## Writing a rubric for each competency

A radar chart becomes meaningful when each axis has a rubric.

Use the following structure:

### Competency name
What the skill is

### Observable definition
What someone actually does

### 1–5 performance scale
Describe what each level looks like in practice

### Evidence
What would show the student is at that level?

### Action steps
What would help the student improve to the next level?

Example:

| Competency | 3D Modeling |
|-----------|-------------|
| Observable definition | Creates clean, usable 3D assets that fit project constraints and production workflow |
| 1 | Produces basic forms with limited control over scale, proportion, or structure |
| 2 | Builds simple models with some attention to form and readability |
| 3 | Creates functional models with clear structure, proportion, and reasonable efficiency |
| 4 | Produces polished models with attention to form, asset needs, and production constraints |
| 5 | Builds and iterates high-quality models for real-world project pipelines while solving technical and aesthetic issues independently |
| Evidence | Portfolio assets, model files, critiques, instructor feedback, project documentation |
| Next action | Practice retopology and optimize assets for real-time use |

This is what makes the chart useful: it converts a visual impression into a plan for growth.

## Recursive breakdown: turning one axis into another radar

Once a student has a major chart, they should select one or two axes that are especially important or uncertain.

### Example: 3D Modeling as a major axis

Main role: Game Designer / Technical Artist

Main radar axis: 3D Modeling

Sub-radar axes:
- Form and proportion
- Topology and structure
- UV mapping
- Texture application
- Asset optimization
- Workflow integration

Each of these becomes a new competency axis with a rubric. The original chart remains the high-level map; the recursive chart explains the internal structure of one key capability.

This is the key recursive move:

- Chart A = role-level synthesis
- Chart B = detailed breakdown of one major competency
- Chart C = application-specific or tool-specific breakdown if needed

This approach allows students to go from broad strategy to specific evidence and action.

## Example of a recursive hierarchy

### Role: Technical Artist

Main radar chart:
- Visual Design
- 3D Production
- Technical Problem Solving
- Collaboration
- Real-Time Workflow
- Research and Iteration
- Communication
- Portfolio Evidence

Sub-radar for 3D Production:
- Modeling
- Retopology
- UVs
- Texturing
- Material Setup
- Optimization
- Asset Pipeline

Sub-radar for Optimization:
- Poly budget awareness
- Real-time performance constraints
- File organization
- Import/export workflow
- Iterative testing

This allows students to see that a single broad category is not a skill in isolation. It is a cluster of competencies that can be measured and improved separately.

## How to link the chart to action and growth

A radar chart should not end at assessment alone. Each chart should lead to an action plan.

Students should answer:
- Which axes are strongest?
- Which axes are most vulnerable?
- Which axes are needed for the next stage of the target role?
- Which sub-skills need direct improvement before an internship, portfolio submission, or career transition?

A good chart is one that produces clear next actions such as:
- complete one project in a specific software tool,
- add a portfolio artifact documenting one key skill,
- seek a collaborator or mentor in a functional area,
- build a short demonstrative skill set tied to a real job description,
- or revise the resume to reflect the competencies with evidence.

---

## Periodic review and revision

Radar charts should be treated as living assessment tools. Students should review them at regular checkpoints—after projects, before portfolio updates, before resume revisions, and before major decision points such as internships, applications, or strategic pivots.

At each review, students should ask:
- What new evidence has changed my score?
- Which axes are now clearer or more specific?
- Which decisions require a deeper sub-radar?
- Which competencies still need action, and which are now stable enough to move forward?

This periodic review practice keeps the chart honest and useful. It also supports the course’s broader emphasis on iterative growth rather than one-time evaluation.

---

## Common mistakes to avoid

### 1. Too many axes
A radar chart with 12–15 axes becomes difficult to interpret. Keep the role-level chart to 5–8 major competencies.

### 2. Vague categories
If the axis cannot be defined in observable terms, it is not yet a useful competency.

### 3. No evidence behind the ratings
Scores should come from projects, portfolio work, research, or direct experience.

### 4. No recursive structure
Students often stop at the first chart, even when one axis is clearly complex enough to deserve its own breakdown.

### 5. Treating the chart as a fixed truth
A radar chart is a planning tool, not a permanent judgment. It should be updated as students learn, revise, and gain evidence.

## Suggested output format for students

Students should submit a document that includes:

1. Role-level radar chart
2. A short explanation of why these competencies matter
3. 1–2 recursive sub-radars for key axes
4. A rubric table for each major competency and each sub-competency
5. A current score and target score for each axis
6. A short action plan for continued growth

This output makes the chart useful in class, in portfolio narratives, and in job-search contexts.

---

## Assessment criteria for the chart

A strong radar chart should demonstrate:
- clarity of purpose,
- specific and observable competencies,
- a justified hierarchy from broad role to sub-skills,
- meaningful rubric language,
- evidence-informed scoring,
- and a realistic action plan.

---

## Connection to the larger course

This process supports the course’s broader goals:
- it helps students move from speculation to targeted preparation,
- it allows them to convert emerging interests into measurable skills,
- it strengthens resume, portfolio, and interview language,
- and it introduces a professional model for continuing education and growth.

In other words, radar charts are not just visual exercises. They are tools for making future direction more concrete, more defensible, and more actionable.
