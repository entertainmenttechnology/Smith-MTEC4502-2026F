**Course:** MTEC 4502 – Career and Portfolio Seminar  
**Instructor:** Dr. David B. Smith  
**Due:** As assigned in the course schedule for the portfolio/pitch unit

## Objective

Prepare a concise, persuasive professional introduction that communicates who you are, what you are focused on, and why you would be valuable to a specific person or decision-maker. This assignment complements the online portfolio and helps you practice the short-form communication professionals use in networking, interviews, pitch meetings, and quick introductions.

This is not just a spoken exercise. Students must also design a single visual slide that supports and extends the spoken message. The slide should function as a visual companion to the pitch, helping the listener understand an integrated professional story across verbal, visual, and textual communication.

You will deliver:

1. A 30-second elevator pitch
2. A single-slide introduction or visual support for that pitch

---

## Deliverables

### 1. Elevator Pitch (30 seconds)

Deliver a spoken pitch lasting approximately 30 seconds. It should clearly answer:

- Who you are
- What you are focused on
- Why you would be valuable to this person or organization
- What you want to happen next

Choose a relevant audience such as a hiring manager, employer representative, founder, graduate advisor, creative technologist, or another decision-maker aligned with your goals.

If you choose a CEO, founder, or other senior leader, briefly identify why that person is the appropriate audience and how you would determine that they are the right decision-maker to address.

### 2. Single Slide (1 slide only)

Create a single-slide visual to support your pitch. The slide should be clean, readable, and easy to understand in under 10 seconds. It should function as a visual storytelling aid that supports, not repeats, your spoken pitch.

The slide should help present an integrated story about who you are, what you do, and why you are valuable. Consider how the visual and textual content work together across multiple communication modalities.

Include only the most important information:

- Your professional identity or role focus
- One or two proof points (project, skill, achievement, or outcome)
- A clear value proposition
- Optional: your target role or area of interest
- A simple visual element or layout structure that helps the audience read the message quickly

Keep the design simple and intentional. The goal is to support your verbal pitch, not to replace it. The slide should reinforce your narrative through layout, hierarchy, emphasis, and concise wording.

---

## Recommended Structure

Use the following model from the planning guide:

1. **Hook:** Introduce yourself and your current focus.
2. **Value / Proof:** Explain what you do and provide one concrete proof point.
3. **Differentiator:** State why your approach, skill set, or background is valuable.
4. **Call to Action:** End with a brief invitation to continue the conversation.

For the slide, translate this same structure into a visual hierarchy:

- **Top / left:** Who you are and your role focus
- **Center:** The strongest proof point or project outcome
- **Right / bottom:** Why your value matters to this audience
- **Footer / minimal text:** A clear action or next step

A strong pitch should sound conversational, confident, and tailored to the listener—not memorized or overly generic. The slide should visually reinforce the same narrative using hierarchy, contrast, and concise text.

## Different Use Cases

Although the pitch and the slide are presented together in class as an integrated exercise, they are not the same professional tool and they are useful in different situations.

- **Use the spoken pitch** when the goal is a quick, conversational introduction in a networking setting, career fair, hallway conversation, coffee chat, or brief virtual meeting.
- **Use the single slide** when the goal is to anchor a short meeting, recruiter screen, team introduction, or a visual summary that supports a brief explanation.
- **Use both together** when a fast spoken introduction needs a visual cue to reinforce credibility, provide proof, or clarify your role and value.

The two forms are complementary, but they are not interchangeable. A spoken pitch is adaptive and conversational; a single slide is structured, visual, and designed for quick comprehension.

---

## Evaluation Criteria

- Clarity: The listener can quickly understand who you are and why you matter.
- Relevance: The pitch is tailored to a specific audience and goal.
- Evidence: You include credible proof points rather than vague claims.
- Conciseness: The pitch is within 30 seconds and remains focused.
- Delivery: Your speaking pace, emphasis, and body language make the pitch persuasive and professional.
- Visual integration: The single slide supports the spoken message through clear layout, hierarchy, and concise wording.
- Multimodal communication: The content works across spoken, visual, and textual forms as one coherent professional story.
- Visual support: The single slide reinforces rather than distracts from your message.
