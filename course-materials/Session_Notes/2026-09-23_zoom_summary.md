### Converting Evidence into Strategy: Discussion and Provocation

David led a discussion on how evidence gathered through research and visualization is transformed into a career strategy, distinguishing strategy from tactics and warning against "miracle thinking."

- **Details**
  - David: Introduced the concept of "miracle thinking" — assuming a gap will close without defining the specific steps to close it, illustrated by the "then a miracle occurs" cartoon.
  - Student F: Suggested using evidence as a foundation for a step-by-step plan.
  - Student D: Proposed talking to people with the desired skills and taking relevant courses as a strategy.
  - David: Clarified that talking to experts is still evidence-gathering; it becomes strategy when specific actionable steps are identified (e.g., a recommended certification course).
  - Student A: Suggested creating metrics and grading oneself against structured goals as a way to build strategy from evidence.
  - David: Defined the distinction — strategy is what you want to achieve; tactics are the specific, actionable steps taken to achieve it.
  - David: Emphasized that strategies must be tested and validated; no plan survives unchanged (referencing Clausewitz: "No plan survives the battlefield").
- **Conclusion**
  - Evidence alone does not constitute a strategy; actionable, connected steps (tactics) are required.
  - Assumptions embedded in visualizations and analyses must be tested and validated.
  - Plans must remain flexible and subject to continuous revision as circumstances change.

  - Job postings reveal deeper questions about what specific competency levels mean in practice.

### Strategy vs. Tactics: A Fabrication Case Study

David used Student D's fabrication/woodworking skill gap as a concrete example to illustrate the difference between strategy and tactics, and the importance of breaking broad skills into subcategories.

- **Details**
  - David: Used woodworking as a proxy example — knowing you need to improve is not a strategy; identifying the specific sub-skills (workflow, tool safety, joinery techniques, etc.) and how to acquire them is tactical planning.
  - Student D: Acknowledged familiarity with most tools but identified safe, organized workflow as the real gap.
  - David: Distinguished between "dabbling" (exploratory, passion-driven) and professional competency (systematic, comprehensive).
  - Student A: Asked how to distinguish between a genuine passion and a skill being developed purely to solve a problem.
  - David: Responded that dabbling is inherently self-motivated; it becomes a career when you commit to mastering all aspects, including those you find less interesting.
- **Conclusion**
  - Broad skill categories must be decomposed into specific sub-skills before tactics can be defined.
  - Professional competency requires mastering all components of a skill area, not just preferred ones.
  - Career commitment means accepting responsibility for the full scope of a role, including less enjoyable tasks.

### Introduction to AI as a Collaborative Tool

David introduced a framework for using AI as a collaborator (iterative, questioning) rather than a tool (directive, one-way), with emphasis on verifying AI outputs.

- **Details**
  - David: Distinguished between AI as a tool (give it a task, it executes) and AI as a collaborator (iterative dialogue, mutual questioning).
  - David: Established two core rules: (1) Do not use AI output you do not understand; (2) Always verify AI claims against reliable sources.
  - David: Categorized AI outputs as supported (source provided and verifiable), unverified (no source but plausible), or unsupported (incorrect or incomplete).
  - David: Shared a personal anecdote about a New York Times article containing several factual errors, illustrating that even reputable sources contain inaccuracies.
  - David: Warned that LLMs are trained largely on internet data, which contains a significant volume of incorrect information.
  - David: Recommended pushing back on AI responses, asking for sources, and testing claims directly (e.g., trying a suggested software shortcut to verify it works).
  - David: Noted AI tends to be agreeable and validating; students must actively challenge its responses.
- **Conclusion**
  - AI should be used as a questioning and investigative partner, not a content generator for this assignment.
  - All AI-generated claims must be verified through independent sources.
  - Critical evaluation of all information sources — including AI, newspapers, and internet content — is a professional responsibility.
